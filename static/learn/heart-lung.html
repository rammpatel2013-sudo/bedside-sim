Heart–lung interactions

One positive-pressure breath changes pleural pressure, lung volume, venous return and both ventricles' afterload. The arterial line records the result as pulse pressure variation. Change the patient and the ventilator, then test the prediction against a fluid bolus.

    Two pressures from one breath

    A positive-pressure breath raises alveolar pressure, and the lungs and chest wall share it in proportion to their stiffness. The rise in pleural pressure equals the tidal volume divided by chest-wall compliance. The rise in transpulmonary pressure equals the tidal volume divided by lung compliance. A 560 mL breath against a chest wall of 100 mL/cmH₂O raises pleural pressure by about 5.6 cmH₂O. Stiff lungs take more of the pressure across the lung itself, and a stiff chest wall or a tense abdomen passes more of it to the heart. Pleural pressure acts on the heart and great veins; transpulmonary pressure sets lung volume, which loads the right ventricle.1

    Venous return and the right ventricle

    Blood returns to the right atrium down a pressure gradient: mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf) minus right atrial pressure, divided by the resistance to venous return.5 Inspiration raises pleural and right atrial pressure, so the gradient and venous return fall. In the model a small share of the rise is passed upstream, as the descending diaphragm compresses the abdominal veins.

    The Venous return plate shows this as two lines that meet. The cardiac function curve slides right as pleural pressure rises; where it crosses the venous return line sets that beat's output. When the heart works on the steep part of its curve, the breath moves output a lot. On the flat part, the same breath barely moves it. That difference is what pulse pressure variation detects.

    Lung volume and right ventricular afterload

    Lung inflation stretches the alveolar vessels, and pulmonary vascular resistance rises with lung volume. In ventilated patients the right ventricle's outflow impedance rises with each inspiration, driven by tidal volume more than by airway pressure.6 A normal right ventricle tolerates this. A failing one loses stroke volume on every breath whatever its preload, which gives a large PPV in a patient who will not respond to fluid.12 Choose Acute cor pulmonale and run the bolus to see it.

    The left ventricle

    Early in inspiration, lung inflation pushes blood from the pulmonary capillaries toward the left atrium, and the rise in pleural pressure lowers the transmural pressure the left ventricle ejects against.1 Both raise left ventricular output for a beat or two, the Δup of systolic pressure. A few beats later the smaller right ventricular output of inspiration reaches the left ventricle, and its stroke volume falls, usually during expiration: the Δdown. The stroke volume strip on the monitor shows the delay, with the RV bars leading the LV bars.

    In hypovolemia Δdown dominates. A failing left ventricle responds to afterload rather than preload, so in LV failure Δup dominates and Δdown nearly disappears. This is the physiology behind the benefit of positive pressure in cardiogenic pulmonary edema.1

    Reading the arterial line

    Pulse pressure variation compares the largest and smallest pulse pressures within one breath, averaged over several breaths:

    PPV = (PPmax − PPmin) ÷ [(PPmax + PPmin) ÷ 2] × 100

    In septic, ventilated patients, a PPV of 13% separated those whose cardiac index rose by 15% or more with fluid from those whose did not.7 In a large cohort under general anesthesia, values from 9% to 13% formed a gray zone that held about a quarter of patients, where the test does not decide.8 Stroke volume variation from a pulse contour device carries the same information. PPV asks whether both ventricles are preload-responsive, and it answers only when the stimulus, the breath, is regular and large enough.

    When PPV misleads

    • Spontaneous efforts make the pleural swing irregular. PPV can be high without preload dependence.13,14
    • Arrhythmia makes stroke volume vary with filling time, not with the breath.13
    • Tidal volume below 8 mL/kg PBW gives too small a stimulus, so PPV underestimates responsiveness.9
    • Heart rate to respiratory rate ratio below 3.6 leaves too few beats per breath; the swing is averaged out in the pulmonary circulation.10
    • Respiratory system compliance of 30 mL/cmH₂O or less lowers its accuracy; leg raising and end-expiratory occlusion held up better.11
    • Right ventricular dysfunction lets afterload, not preload, make the swing.12
    • Raised intra-abdominal pressure enlarges the pleural swing and inflates PPV; an open chest shrinks it.13

    Tests that do not rely on PPV

    Passive leg raising moves venous blood from the legs and abdomen into the chest and reverses itself when the legs come down. A rise in cardiac output of 10% or more predicts fluid responsiveness well; judged by pulse pressure alone, the test performs worse.15

    The tidal volume challenge raises tidal volume from 6 to 8 mL/kg PBW for one minute. In patients ventilated with low tidal volumes, an absolute rise in PPV of 3.5 points or in SVV of 2.5 points predicted responsiveness, while baseline PPV did not.16

    End-expiratory occlusion stops the ventilator at end-expiration for 15 seconds and removes the cyclic impediment to venous return. A rise in cardiac output of 5% or more predicts responsiveness.17 In this model the occlusion also removes the cyclic load on the right ventricle, so a failing right ventricle can give a positive occlusion test without preload reserve.

    Run each test on the presets, then run the bolus. The bolus is the model's own answer; the tests are predictions of it.

    About the model

    A teaching model, set to agree with the sources in direction and rough size. It is not patient data and is not for clinical decisions. Each beat, venous return (Pmsf − right atrial pressure) ÷ resistance meets a right ventricular function curve of transmural pressure, whose plateau falls as transpulmonary pressure rises. The pulmonary circulation is one reservoir between the ventricles, which makes the left ventricle lag. The left ventricle follows a Starling curve of left atrial transmural pressure and gains output as pleural pressure lowers its afterload, more so when it is weak. Arteries are a two-element windkessel. Fixed values: 15% of the pleural rise passed upstream to Pmsf; pleural pressure −3 cmH₂O at end-expiration without PEEP; I:E 1:2; predicted body weight 70 kg; leg raise treated as a 300 mL transfer and the bolus as 500 mL, each turned into a Pmsf rise through a systemic compliance of 125 mL/mmHg. A responder is a stroke volume rise of 15% or more with the bolus. Numbers are graded after rounding to the shown decimal.
