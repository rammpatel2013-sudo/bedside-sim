In brief

The AHA, ACC and eight partner societies published their first acute PE guideline in February 2026. It drops “massive” and “submassive” for five clinical categories, A through E, each split into subcategories, with an R flag added when breathing is the dominant problem. A patient with normotensive shock on a non-rebreather is now a “D2R.”

Three things matter most at the bedside. No reperfusion option is rated above 2a, even in cardiogenic shock, and systemic lysis no longer outranks catheter therapy. Everything in the gray zone between C3 and D2 is a 2b, because the outcome trials had not reported by the April 2025 evidence cutoff; HI-PEITHO has since reported a positive clinical endpoint in that zone. And the supportive care is now explicit: enoxaparin over a heparin drip, PERT for C–E, high-flow oxygen, vasopressors for shock, and a Harm grade on deep sedation and intubation.

This JCVA paper is a Mayo multidisciplinary group’s expert read of that guideline. Its full text is paywalled and it carries no abstract, so this brief is built from the guideline itself. The authors’ own judgments are not represented here.

Focus

The guideline covers adults (18 years and older) from the onset of symptoms through follow-up at a year: pretest probability and imaging, severity assignment, where to treat, anticoagulant choice and duration, reperfusion, ICU support, filters, and screening for chronic thromboembolic disease. Its organizing device is the new AHA/ACC Acute PE Clinical Categories. Patients are assigned by their single worst clinical, laboratory or imaging finding, and they are expected to move between categories on reassessment.

What the perspective engages. Without the text, the only visible signal is its 131-item reference list. It cites the guideline in both journals alongside the 2019 ESC guideline and an inter-guideline comparison (Zuin, JACC 2024). The remaining clusters are risk granularity (SCAI shock stages, the CPES score, venous lactate, MAP thresholds, shock index, NEWS), anticoagulant pharmacology including bivalirudin, argatroban and direct thrombin inhibitor dosing on ECMO, reduced-dose extended DOACs, and mechanical thrombectomy versus catheter lysis (PEERLESS plus registry and readmission data). It also cites PERT meta-analyses and AI-triggered PERT activation, VA-ECMO series, the group’s own work on thrombectomy safety outside the OR, IVC filters, and CTEPH. What the authors conclude on any of these is not visible without the full text.

Why it exists

Until February 2026, North American clinicians had no comprehensive guideline for acute PE management; the CHEST antithrombotic guidelines covered anticoagulation only. Clinicians worked from the 2011 AHA scientific statement (Jaff), which sorted patients into massive, submassive and low-risk, and borrowed the 2019 ESC guideline, which split intermediate risk into low and high by RV findings and troponin. Both schemes left the same problem unsolved. “Intermediate-high risk” holds patients who are comfortable on room air alongside patients in occult shock with a lactate of 4. Neither scheme had a slot for the patient whose main problem is hypoxemia rather than blood pressure.

Meanwhile practice moved faster than evidence. Catheter-directed lysis and large-bore thrombectomy spread through US hospitals on the strength of single-arm studies and RV/LV surrogates, and PERTs multiplied with mixed outcome data. Ten societies, spanning cardiology, emergency medicine, chest medicine, hospital medicine, interventional radiology, vascular medicine and nursing, and pharmacy, convened to write one common reference. The literature search ran February to October 2024, with selected key studies added through April 2025. That cutoff matters, because three randomized trials of catheter therapy against anticoagulation alone reported within a year after it.

What changed & why

This is a de novo guideline, so “prior version” here means the 2011 AHA statement and the 2019 ESC guideline that North American practice leaned on.

Severity language
WasMassive / submassive / low risk (AHA 2011); high / intermediate-high / intermediate-low / low (ESC 2019)
NowA–E with nine subcategories, an R modifier, and named pre-failure states (transient hypotension, normotensive shock)
The intermediate tier was too broad to guide therapy. Splitting out D captures patients who are hypoperfused but not yet hypotensive. The FLASH registry found isolated hypoperfusion in 34% of intermediate-risk patients undergoing thrombectomy. The R modifier recognizes presentations driven by gas exchange rather than blood pressure.
Systemic lysis in shock
WasESC 2019: recommended for high-risk PE (Class I, B), with catheter therapy and surgery as fallbacks when lysis fails or is contraindicated
Now2a (C-LD) in E1 and E2, the same class as catheter lysis, thrombectomy and surgery in E1
The randomized base for lysis in shock is four small trials totaling 224 patients. The ICH cost is real: one meta-analysis reported severe bleeding of 9.9% and ICH of 1.7%. Nonrandomized catheter data have matured; in FLAME, in-hospital mortality was 1.9% with thrombectomy against 29.5% with other therapies, though that comparison was not randomized. The guideline flattens the hierarchy rather than reversing it.
Reperfusion without shock
WasESC 2019: routine primary lysis not recommended in intermediate or low risk (III, B); rescue lysis on deterioration
NowGraded by category: lysis is Harm in A1–C2, and every modality is 2b in C3 and D
PEITHO (2014) showed prevention of collapse paid for with bleeding and stroke. Meta-analyses in C3–D2-like patients show lower mortality with a matching ICH excess. The 2b is an honest statement that the catheter trials were still running at the cutoff.
First anticoagulant in sick patients
WasESC 2019: LMWH preferred generally, but UFH started without delay in high-risk PE and customary for anyone who might go to lysis
NowLMWH over UFH from C1 through E1 (1, B-R), including after procedures (1) and when one is likely (2a); E2 either
A meta-analysis found less recurrent VTE with LMWH without more major bleeding. LMWH has predictable kinetics, needs no titration, and causes less HIT. A UFH drip that sits subtherapeutic for a day is a quiet hazard that the “in case we lyse” logic tends to ignore.
Team, circuit, airway
WasESC 2019: multidisciplinary teams “should be considered” (IIa, C); ECMO “may be considered” (IIb, C); no graded airway guidance
NowPERT Class 1 for C–E; VA-ECMO 2a in E2; HFNC 2a; deep sedation and intubation 3: Harm; induction readiness Class 1
Observational evidence for PERT and ECMO grew, though PERT’s mortality effect is still not established. The airway grades codify RV physiology: induction removes the sympathetic tone that holds up a failing RV, and positive pressure raises its afterload.
Follow-up and the long tail
WasESC 2019: routine reassessment at 3–6 months; reduced-dose extended DOAC “should be considered” (IIa, A)
NowA one-week touchpoint, a visit by 3 months, a symptom screen at every visit for a year; half-dose extended apixaban or rivaroxaban Class 1, A
Early touchpoints catch bleeding, adherence and access problems. Structured screening catches CTEPD, which is otherwise found late and is treatable at expert centers.
Bottom line

The guideline gives everyone at the bedside, from ED to cath lab to ICU, a common and finer severity language. It is deliberately modest about reperfusion. No modality rises above 2a even in shock, and the entire C3–D zone sits at 2b because the outcome trials had not reported by April 2025.

That 2b is already dated at the edges. HI-PEITHO (2026) showed that ultrasound-facilitated catheter lysis cut early decompensation in a C3–D2-type population without ICH, though the effect leaned on a NEWS-based endpoint. The most durable, immediately usable content is the supportive care: enoxaparin rather than a drip, lactate and RV assessment for everyone in C and above, PERT, high-flow oxygen, norepinephrine with early vasopressin, and protecting the RV from the induction drug.

At the bedside
  • Document itWrite the category in the admission note and the PERT call (“C3R”, “D2”). It tells the interventionalist, the ICU and the next shift the same thing, and it forces a re-score at 24–72 h, when deterioration clusters.
  • Order setFor C and above: lactate, troponin and/or BNP, RV assessment by CT or echo, and a PERT page. In C3, treat a MAP below 80 as a reason to watch closely. In a normotensive patient, a lactate above 2, falling urine output, rising creatinine or low cardiac index means D2, not C3.
  • AnticoagulantStart enoxaparin, even if catheter therapy is on the table. If your order sets default to a UFH drip “in case we lyse,” this guideline argues the other way except in E2. Skip routine anti-Xa levels. If you need one, for example in severe CKD, draw a peak 3–5 h after the third or later dose.
  • HypotensionStart norepinephrine. Around 15 µg/min, add vasopressin rather than climbing, because higher norepinephrine doses raise PVR. Add dobutamine for low output. Give volume only in small boluses of 500 mL or less, when preload depletion is genuinely suspected. An inhaled pulmonary vasodilator is a 2b option.
  • AirwayPut the patient on HFNC early. Avoid intubation if at all possible. If it is unavoidable, use a hemodynamically neutral induction with a vasopressor running and an inotrope drawn up, and alert the ECMO team and the cath lab or OR before the induction drug goes in.
  • E1 / E2For E1, choose among lysis, catheter therapy and surgery by bleeding risk and by what your center can do fastest. For E2, consider VA-ECMO as the bridge (2a), with lysis an alternative, and keep the patient anticoagulated on the circuit.
  • DischargeArrange contact within a week and a visit by 3 months. Ask about breathlessness at each visit for a year. For an unprovoked first PE, plan extended anticoagulation, with half-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban after the initial phase.
Where it fits — the research around it

The lineage of risk

The 2011 AHA scientific statement (Jaff) gave us massive and submassive. The 2019 ESC guideline (Konstantinides) layered sPESI (Jiménez, 2010), RV imaging and troponin into four tiers, and made systemic lysis Class I for high-risk PE. Hestia and PESI-based home treatment, tested in trials such as Aujesky’s outpatient RCT and the 114-patient US Hestia/troponin trial, underpin the A/B “go home” recommendations. The D and E definitions borrow directly from SCAI cardiogenic shock staging (2019, updated 2022). The normotensive-shock concept comes from cardiogenic-shock work dating to the SHOCK trial (1999).

Lysis: the trade that defines C3

PEITHO (2014, 1,006 patients, tenecteplase) is the anchor. It prevented hemodynamic collapse, but major extracranial bleeding was 6.3% against 1.2%, and stroke 2.4% against 0.2%. Its population maps onto C3–D2. Rescue lysis worked for patients who decompensated on heparin, which favors watchful waiting over immediate lysis. A meta-analysis of 16 trials (2,115 patients) the guideline cites found lower all-cause mortality (OR 0.53, NNT 59) at the price of ICH (OR 4.6, NNH 78). MOPETT (2013) and later low-dose regimens support the 2b nod to reduced-dose lysis, pending PEITHO-3.

Catheters: from surrogate to outcome

ULTIMA (2014), SEATTLE II (2015) and OPTALYSE (2018) established that catheter lysis improves RV/LV ratio at small lytic doses. FLARE (2019), EXTRACT-PE (2021) and the 1,000-patient FLASH registry did the same for thrombectomy without any lytic. FLAME (2023) brought nonrandomized high-risk data. PEERLESS (2025, 550 patients in C2–D2) compared thrombectomy with catheter lysis. It found no difference in 30-day mortality or major bleeding, but more deterioration and bailout with catheter lysis. That is why choosing between the two remains a 2b.

The guideline’s cutoff fell just before the first trials against anticoagulation alone. STORM-PE (TCT 2025, 100 patients) showed a larger 48-hour RV/LV reduction with computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (0.52 versus 0.24); that is still a surrogate. HI-PEITHO (NEJM 2026, 544 patients with RV/LV ≥1, raised troponin and at least two signs of distress) is the first clinical-endpoint win. PE death, decompensation or recurrence at 7 days fell from 10.3% to 4.0% (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.20–0.77). There was no ICH, and major bleeding was 4.1% against 2.2% (not significant). Caveats: the trial was open-label and device-sponsored, decompensation could be triggered by NEWS ≥9 alone, and 30-day mortality was 1.8% against 1.1%. Its patients are the guideline’s C3–D2 “may be considered” group.

Anticoagulation, filters, the long tail

PREPIC (1998) and PREPIC2 (2015) are why a routine filter in an anticoagulated patient is now graded Harm. AMPLIFY-EXT (2013) and EINSTEIN CHOICE (2017) support Class 1 half-dose extended therapy. The guideline’s structured screening for CTEPD reflects how often post-PE dyspnea goes uninvestigated.
